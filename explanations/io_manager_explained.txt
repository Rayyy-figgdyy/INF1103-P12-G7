IO_MANAGER.PY EXPLAINED
A plain-language guide for teammates who don't code much

This guide walks through io_manager.py, the part of our Car Maintenance &
Service Risk Tracker that talks to the user. You do not need to know Python to
follow it. Code names are shown indented by 4 spaces, like this:

    prompt_km

All examples use real values from our app: the plate SBA1234A, a Honda Civic
2025, Engine Oil serviced every 10,000 km or 12 months, and "today" being
12 October 2026.


WHAT THIS FILE IS FOR

io_manager.py is the screen and keyboard of the app. Everything the user sees
on screen and everything the user types passes through this file, and nowhere
else. It shows the menus, asks the questions, checks the answers, and shows the
results.

Think of it as the receptionist at a car workshop. The receptionist greets you,
hands you the forms, checks you filled them in properly ("this date is in the
future, please fix it"), and later reads the mechanic's report back to you.
The receptionist never fixes the car, never files the paperwork in the back
room, and never decides what repair you need. Those jobs belong to other files.

The most important habit of this file is "reject and re-prompt": if the user
types something wrong, it explains what is wrong and asks the same question
again. The app never crashes because of a typing mistake.


WHERE IT FITS

Our app has four "manager" files plus main.py, which wires them together:

    io_manager    ->  ai_manager  ->  logic_manager  ->  data_manager
    (screen and       (asks the       (business         (saves and loads
     keyboard)         AI)             rules)            the files)

main.py is the conductor. It calls io_manager to get answers from the user,
passes those answers on to the AI and the business rules, saves the result with
data_manager, and finally hands the finished record back to io_manager to show
on screen.

What io_manager RECEIVES from main.py:

1. Lists to choose from, e.g. the 4 car models or the 6 parts (main.py reads
   them from cars.json).
2. Saved facts it is not allowed to look up itself, e.g. the set of plates
   already registered, or this car's highest odometer reading on file.
3. Finished records to display, e.g. a maintenance check with the AI's
   urgency score and the final outcome already filled in.
4. One-line messages to print, e.g. "Goodbye!" (main.py is not allowed to
   print, so it asks io_manager to do it).

What io_manager HANDS BACK to main.py:

1. Clean, already-checked answers, e.g. the plate "SBA1234A", the number 70800,
   or the date "2026-03-15".
2. Nothing at all for the display functions: they only print.

io_manager never saves files, never talks to the AI, and never makes business
decisions such as "is this part urgent?".


WALKTHROUGH

The file is in seven parts, and this walkthrough follows the same order:

1. Fixed settings (menus, the plate pattern, the date format)
2. Small helpers that convert or tidy values
3. Questions that keep asking until the answer is valid
4. The main menu
5. Account screens (login, register, update, show profile)
6. Maintenance check questions
7. Screens that show results


Part 1: Fixed settings

These are values written once at the top and never changed while the app runs.
Their names are in CAPITALS to show that.

    MENU_LOGGED_OUT
The menu shown when nobody is logged in: 1) Login, 2) Register new profile,
3) Exit.

    MENU_LOGGED_IN
The menu shown when someone is logged in, with options 1 to 8 (check a part,
history, urgent items, view/update/delete profile, logout, exit). Its top line
has three gaps, {name}, {plate} and {car_model}, which are filled in later, so
it reads for example "Logged in: Darren", then "SBA1234A", then
"Honda Civic 2025", separated by vertical bars.

    LICENSE_PLATE_PATTERN
The shape a Singapore plate must have: 1 to 3 capital letters, then 1 to 4
digits, then 1 capital letter, and nothing else. SBA1234A fits. SBA1234 (no
final letter) does not.

    MAX_ODOMETER_KM
Set to 2,000,000. Any odometer reading above this is treated as a typing
mistake.

    DATE_FORMAT and DATE_HINT
Users type and see dates as DD/MM/YYYY, e.g. 12/10/2026. Behind the scenes
the app SAVES dates as YYYY-MM-DD, e.g. 2026-10-12, because that style sorts
correctly and is easy to calculate with. DATE_HINT is the text "DD/MM/YYYY"
shown to the user in questions.


Part 2: Small helpers

    parse_date(typed)
What it does: turns a date the user typed into a real date the computer
understands. Dashes are accepted as well as slashes.
Needs: the typed text.
Gives back: a real date, or "None" (meaning "nothing") if it is not a real
date.
Example: "12/10/2026" and "12-10-2026" both become 12 October 2026.
"31/02/2025" gives None, because February has no 31st. "hello" also gives None.

    show_date(saved_date)
What it does: the opposite of parse_date, for the screen. It turns a saved
YYYY-MM-DD date into DD/MM/YYYY.
Needs: a saved date (or nothing).
Gives back: text for the screen.
Example: "2026-10-12" is shown as "12/10/2026". If there is no date at all it
shows "-". If the saved value is damaged, e.g. "2026-13-45", it shows that
value unchanged instead of crashing.

    km(value)
What it does: makes a distance easy to read by adding thousands commas.
Needs: a number of km. It also copes with a number saved as text.
Gives back: text for the screen.
Example: 70800 becomes "70,800". A decimal like 49000.4 is rounded to
"49,000". The text "52000" becomes "52,000". A missing value shows "-". Junk
like "abc" is shown as "abc" rather than crashing the history screen.

    normalise_plate(typed)
What it does: tidies up a typed plate so small differences don't matter. It
removes spaces and dashes and makes every letter a capital.
Needs: the plate exactly as typed.
Gives back: the tidy plate.
Example: "sba 1234-a" becomes "SBA1234A".


Part 3: Questions that keep asking until the answer is valid

Each of these asks a question, checks the answer, and if the answer is wrong
prints a message starting with "->" and asks again. It only finishes once the
answer is acceptable.

    prompt_text(label)
What it does: asks for any text that is not empty.
Needs: the question label, e.g. "Name".
Gives back: the answer with spaces trimmed from both ends.
Example: pressing Enter without typing shows

    -> Name cannot be empty. Please try again.

and asks again. Typing "  Darren " gives back "Darren".

    prompt_km(label)
What it does: asks for a whole number of kilometres. Commas are allowed.
Needs: the question label, e.g. "Odometer at last service (km)".
Gives back: the whole number.
Example: "60,000" gives back 60000. Answers with letters, a minus sign, a
decimal point, or nothing at all are refused with "must be a whole number of
km, e.g. 45200". A number above 2,000,000 is refused with "looks too large
(max 2,000,000 km)".

    prompt_past_date(label)
What it does: asks for a real date that is today or earlier. Used for the date
of the last service, which cannot be in the future.
Needs: the question label, e.g. "Date of last service".
Gives back: the date in the saved style, YYYY-MM-DD.
Example: on 12/10/2026, typing "15/03/2026" gives back "2026-03-15". Typing
"20/12/2026" is refused with "cannot be in the future". Typing "31/02/2026"
is refused with "must be a real date in DD/MM/YYYY format".

    prompt_number_choice(label, options)
What it does: prints the options as a numbered list starting at 1, then asks
for a number.
Needs: the question label and the list of options.
Gives back: the option the user picked (the words, not the number).
Example: with our 6 parts (main.py hands them over in A to Z order) the
screen shows

     1) Brake Pads
     2) Coolant
     3) Engine Air Filter
     4) Engine Oil
     5) Spark Plugs
     6) Tyres

Typing 9 is refused with "Please enter a number from 1 to 6." Typing 4 gives
back "Engine Oil".

    confirm_action(question)
What it does: asks a yes/no question.
Needs: the question, e.g. "Permanently delete SBA1234A and its 3 maintenance
record(s)?" (main.py writes this question).
Gives back: True for yes, False for no.
Example: "y", "Y", "yes" or "YES" all mean yes. "n" or "no" mean no. Anything
else gets "Please answer y or n." and the question is asked again.

    wait_for_enter()
What it does: shows "Press Enter to go back to the menu..." and waits.
Needs: nothing.
Gives back: nothing.
Why: without this pause the menu would be printed straight after the results
and push them off the top of a small terminal window.


Part 4: The main menu

    prompt_main_menu(current_user)
What it does: shows the logged-out menu if nobody is logged in, otherwise the
logged-in menu with the user's name, plate and car filled in. Then it reads the
user's choice.
Needs: the logged-in user's profile, or None if nobody is logged in.
Gives back: exactly what the user typed (spaces trimmed), e.g. "1".
Note: this function does not check the choice. main.py does that and shows
"Invalid choice. Please enter a number from the menu." if needed.


Part 5: Account screens

    prompt_login()
What it does: shows "--- Login ---" and asks for a license plate. Empty
answers are refused.
Needs: nothing.
Gives back: the tidied plate.
Example: typing "sba1234a" gives back "SBA1234A". main.py then checks whether
that plate is registered.

    prompt_identity(taken_plates)
What it does: the first part of registering. Asks for a name, then a plate.
The plate must fit the Singapore pattern and must not already be registered.
Needs: the set of plates already registered (main.py looks this up, because
this file is not allowed to read the saved files).
Gives back: the name and plate together, e.g. name "Darren" and plate
"SBA1234A".
Example: "SBA1234" is refused because it does not look like SBA1234A. If
SBA1234A is already taken, the user sees "SBA1234A is already registered.
Please try again."

    prompt_car_choice(car_models)
What it does: asks "Which car do you drive?" and shows the supported cars as a
numbered list (using prompt_number_choice).
Needs: the list of car models from cars.json.
Gives back: the chosen model, e.g. "Honda Civic 2025".

    prompt_coe_expiry()
What it does: asks for the COE (Certificate of Entitlement) expiry date.
Needs: nothing.
Gives back: the date in the saved style, e.g. typing 31/05/2030 gives
"2030-05-31".
Note: unlike a service date, a date in the PAST is allowed here. An expired
COE is real information, and our business rules react to it.

    prompt_updated_profile(current)
What it does: lets the user change their name and COE expiry date. The current
value is shown in square brackets, and pressing Enter keeps it. The plate and
car are shown with "(cannot be changed)", because they identify the account.
Needs: the user's current profile.
Gives back: an updated COPY of the profile (main.py saves it).
Example: the screen shows

    Name [Darren]:
    COE expiry date (DD/MM/YYYY) [31/05/2030]:

Pressing Enter on both keeps everything. A badly typed COE date gets "Use
DD/MM/YYYY, or press Enter to keep." and is asked again.

    display_user(user)
What it does: prints the profile: name, plate, car model, car condition (e.g.
"Brand New" or "Used") and COE expiry date (shown as DD/MM/YYYY).
Needs: the user's profile.
Gives back: nothing.
If a detail is missing from the profile, the word None is printed in its place
(or "-" for a missing COE date) instead of the app crashing.


Part 6: Maintenance check questions

    prompt_part_choice(parts)
What it does: asks "Which part are you checking?" and shows the parts as a
numbered list. Only parts with a stored service interval in cars.json are
listed.
Needs: the list of parts for this car.
Gives back: the part name, e.g. "Engine Oil".

    prompt_service_details(last_known_odometer)
What it does: asks for three things. From the last workshop invoice: the date
of the last service and the odometer at that service. Then today's odometer
reading. This is where BUSINESS RULE 1 (input validation) lives: a car's
odometer only goes up, so today's reading is refused if it is lower than
either the reading at the last service, or the last reading on file for this
car.
Needs: the highest odometer reading saved for this car (main.py looks it up
across all parts), or None if the car has no saved checks yet. If there is a
reading, it is shown as a reminder before the question.
Gives back: the three answers together: last service date (YYYY-MM-DD), last
service odometer, and current odometer.
Example: see the worked example below.


Part 7: Screens that show results

All of these read details with a "safe lookup" (explained in the Python ideas
section) and pass dates and distances through show_date and km. So if one old
saved record is missing a detail or has a damaged value, it is still shown
instead of crashing the whole screen.

    display_message(message)
What it does: prints one message for main.py, e.g. "Goodbye!" or
"Welcome back, Darren!".

    display_record(record)
What it does: prints one maintenance check in full, one detail per line: part,
car and plate; check date and odometer; last service; the manufacturer's
schedule; km and days since the last service; the AI's urgency score out of 10
and its confidence; which AI answered (Gemini, or Groq as backup); the AI's
summary and manual reference; the OUTCOME; the "service by" date; the risk
score out of 100; and the advice.
Two details: the "Overdue by" line is only printed for overdue parts, and if
the AI could not give an urgency score it shows "n/a" instead of "6/10".

    display_list(records)
What it does: prints many checks as a neat table, one row each. Used by
"View my maintenance history" and "View my urgent / overdue items".
Columns: Checked (date), Part, Odometer, Urgency, Service by, Outcome.
Part names longer than 19 letters are cut short so the columns stay lined up.
A missing urgency score is shown as "-". If the list is empty it prints
"No records to show."

    display_result(record)
What it does: prints ">>> Assessment saved." and then the full record using
display_record. Used right after a new check.

    display_repeated(previous)
What it does: used when the user types exactly the same details as an earlier
check. main.py spots the repeat; this function prints "This is repeated
information.", explains that it was not sent to the AI again, and shows the
earlier saved assessment using display_record.


WORKED EXAMPLE

Darren (plate SBA1234A, Honda Civic 2025) is logged in on 12/10/2026. His
car's highest saved odometer reading is 69,500 km, from an earlier Brake Pads
check. He chooses 1) Check a part.

Step 1. prompt_part_choice shows the 6 parts. He types 4 and gets
"Engine Oil".

Step 2. main.py looks up his last reading (69,500) and calls
prompt_service_details(69500). The screen goes like this (his typing is after
each colon):

    From your last workshop invoice for this part:
    Date of last service (DD/MM/YYYY): 15/03/2026
    Odometer at last service (km): 60,000
    (Last odometer reading on file for this car: 69,500 km)
    Current odometer reading (km): 59000
      -> Can't be lower than the odometer at last service (60,000 km).
         Please try again.
    Current odometer reading (km): 69000
      -> Can't be lower than the last reading on file (69,500 km).
         Please try again.
    Current odometer reading (km): 70800

(The two "->" messages are each one long line on the real screen.)

What happened:

1. "15/03/2026" passed the date check, because it is a real date and not in
   the future. It is stored as "2026-03-15".
2. "60,000" had its comma removed and became the number 60000.
3. 59000 was refused: it is below the 60,000 km on the invoice.
4. 69000 was refused: it is below the 69,500 km already on file.
5. 70800 passed both checks, so the question stops.

The function hands back: last service date "2026-03-15", last service odometer
60000, current odometer 70800.

Step 3. main.py adds the Engine Oil schedule (10,000 km or 12 months), sends
everything to the AI, applies the business rules, and saves the record. None
of that happens in this file. Suppose the AI replies with urgency 6 and
"Medium" confidence. The rules work out that 10,800 km have been driven since
the service, which is 800 km past the 10,000 km limit, so the part is overdue.

Step 4. main.py calls display_result, and the screen shows:

    >>> Assessment saved.

    --- Maintenance check ---
    Part:               Engine Oil  (Honda Civic 2025, SBA1234A)
    Checked on:         12/10/2026 at 70,800 km
    Last serviced:      15/03/2026 at 60,000 km
    Schedule:           every 10,000 km or 12 months
    Since last service: 10,800 km / 211 days
    Overdue by:         800 km / 0 days
    AI urgency:         6/10 (confidence: Medium)
    Answered by:        Gemini
    AI summary:         (the AI's one-line summary)
    Manual reference:   (where in the manual this comes from)
    OUTCOME:            DUE SOON - Overdue by manufacturer schedule
    Service by:         12/10/2026
    Risk score:         75/100
    Advice:             (the AI's advice)

Notice that every date is DD/MM/YYYY and every distance has commas, thanks to
show_date and km. "Overdue by" appears only because the part is overdue.

Step 5. Later he chooses 2) View my maintenance history, and display_list
shows a table like this:

    Checked     Part                  Odometer  Urgency Service by  Outcome
    12/10/2026  Engine Oil              70,800  6       12/10/2026  DUE SOON...
    01/09/2026  Brake Pads              69,500  -       15/09/2026  NEEDS...

(The outcomes are printed in full on the real screen; they are cut short here
to fit this page. The "-" under Urgency is a check where the AI could not
answer.)


PYTHON IDEAS USED IN THIS FILE

1. import. Borrows ready-made tools that come with Python, like taking a
   toolbox off the shelf. This file borrows datetime (dates) and re (pattern
   checking).

2. Constants. Values written in CAPITALS at the top that never change while the
   app runs, like the opening hours painted on a shop door.

3. Triple-quoted text. Text between three quote marks can span several lines,
   so the menus are written exactly as they look on screen.

4. Functions (def ... return). A function is a named recipe. The brackets list
   the ingredients it needs, and "return" hands the finished dish back to
   whoever asked for it.

5. Type hints. Notes like "-> str" after a function's name tell human readers
   what goes in and what comes out. A vertical bar between two kinds of
   value means "either one", so a dictionary, bar, None means "a dictionary,
   or possibly nothing". Python itself does not enforce these notes.

6. None. Python's word for "nothing here", like an empty box on a form.

7. True and False. The two yes/no values. confirm_action hands back one of
   them.

8. if / elif / else. "If this, do that; else if this other thing, do that;
   otherwise do this." Only the first matching branch runs.

9. while True loops, break and return. A loop repeats the same steps.
   "while True" means "keep going forever", so it only stops when a "return"
   hands back an answer or a "break" jumps out. This is how every question in
   this file keeps asking until it gets a valid answer.

10. for loops and enumerate. A for loop repeats once for each item in a list.
    enumerate(options, start=1) hands out each item with a number, starting
    at 1, like a ticket machine numbering a queue.

11. Lists and counting from 0. A list is items kept in order, like a numbered
    shopping list. Python numbers the positions from 0, so menu choice 1 is
    list position 0. That is why the code subtracts 1. len(list) counts the
    items.

12. Dictionaries. A dictionary is like a form with labelled boxes, e.g. a box
    "name" holding "Darren" and a box "license_plate" holding "SBA1234A".
    user["name"] reads the box labelled "name". dict(current) makes a
    photocopy of the form, so changes don't touch the original until main.py
    saves them.

13. Safe lookup with .get(). user.get("name") reads a box like user["name"],
    but gives back None instead of crashing if the box is missing. Adding a
    second value, as in record.get("ai_provider", "n/a"), gives a fallback to
    use instead of None. It is like asking "is there a name on this form?"
    instead of insisting there must be.

14. Sets and "in". A set is a bag of unique items with no order, very quick to
    search, like a guest list at the door. "plate in taken_plates" asks "is
    this plate on the list?". "in" also works on small fixed groups, as in
    answer in ("y", "yes").

15. try / except. A safety net: "try these steps; if Python complains, do the
    except part instead of crashing". parse_date and show_date use it to deal
    with impossible or damaged dates.

16. Text tools. .strip() trims spaces from both ends. .replace(",", "")
    removes commas. .upper() and .lower() change letters to capitals or small
    letters. .isdigit() is True only if every character is 0 to 9, so it
    refuses minus signs, decimal points, letters and empty answers. int(...)
    turns the text "70800" into the number 70800. str(...) turns a value into
    text.

17. isinstance. Asks "is this value a certain kind of thing?", e.g. "is this a
    number?". The km helper uses it, and deliberately excludes True and False,
    because Python secretly treats them as the numbers 1 and 0.

18. Cutting text with [:19]. Keeps only the first 19 characters, like trimming
    a long label with scissors so it fits its slot.

19. Regular expressions (re). A way to check that text follows a pattern.
    re.compile prepares the pattern once, like cutting a stencil, and .match
    holds a plate against the stencil to see if it fits.

20. Dates. strptime reads typed text using our DD/MM/YYYY stencil. strftime
    writes a date out in that same style. isoformat writes the saved style
    YYYY-MM-DD, and fromisoformat reads it back. datetime.date.today() is
    today's date from the computer's clock. Later dates count as "bigger", so
    "date > today" means "in the future".

21. input and print. input shows a question and waits for the user to type and
    press Enter. print shows text on screen. "\n" inside text starts a new
    line, which is how blank lines appear above headings.

22. f-strings. Text with an f in front, where values are slotted into curly
    brackets, like a mail-merge letter: f"{label}: " with label "Name" prints
    "Name: ".

23. .format(...). Another way to fill gaps. The logged-in menu has gaps named
    {name}, {plate} and {car_model}, and .format fills them with the user's
    details.

24. Joining text pieces. Two pieces of text written next to each other, with
    nothing in between, are glued into one. display_list builds each table row
    this way, one piece per column.

25. Layout codes inside f-strings. These control how a value is laid out, like
    typing into fixed-width boxes on a paper form:

    :<12    left-align in a box 12 characters wide (dates, part names)
    :>10    right-align in a box 10 characters wide, so numbers line up on
            the right like in a bank statement (the Odometer column)
    :>2     right-align in 2 characters, so " 9)" and "10)" line up
    :,      add thousands commas: 70800 becomes 70,800
    :,.0f   add commas and round to a whole number: 49000.4 becomes 49,000

26. Big numbers with underscores. 2_000_000 is just 2000000 written so humans
    can read it, like writing 2,000,000 on paper.


PROJECT RULES THIS FILE FOLLOWS

1. All screen input and output lives here (brief section 2.1). Every print()
   and input() in the app is in this file. Other files that need to say
   something, like main.py, call display_message.

2. Clean values only. The questions hand back checked, tidy values: plates in
   capitals without spaces, distances as whole numbers, dates in the saved
   YYYY-MM-DD style.

3. Reject and re-prompt. Every question that checks its answer keeps asking
   until the answer is valid, with a "->" message saying what was wrong. A
   typing mistake never crashes the app.

4. Business rule 1, input validation. prompt_service_details refuses a current
   odometer lower than the odometer at the last service, or lower than the last
   reading on file for this car. prompt_past_date also refuses a last service
   date in the future, and prompt_km refuses anything that is not a whole
   number or is above 2,000,000 km.

5. Dates: DD/MM/YYYY for people, YYYY-MM-DD for storage. parse_date and the
   question functions convert what is typed into the saved style; show_date
   converts saved dates back for every screen.

6. No file access, no AI, no decisions. When this file needs saved facts (the
   plates already taken, the last odometer on file) main.py looks them up and
   passes them in. Outcomes, risk scores and "service by" dates are worked out
   by logic_manager; this file only displays them. Even the "repeated
   information" check is decided by main.py; this file just shows the message.

7. Required display functions. The brief asks for display_record, display_list
   and display_result, and all three are here.

8. Safe display of damaged records. The display functions use safe lookups and
   the show_date and km helpers, so one missing or damaged saved record cannot
   crash the history or urgent screens.

9. Account identity is fixed. When updating a profile, only the name and COE
   expiry date can change. The plate and car identify the account and are
   shown as "(cannot be changed)".

10. Expired COEs are accepted. prompt_coe_expiry allows past dates on purpose,
    because the business rules have a special outcome for an expired COE.

11. Looking ahead. In Phase 2 of the project (the object-oriented version),
    this file is planned to become an IOManager class with InputHandler and
    OutputHandler sub-classes.
